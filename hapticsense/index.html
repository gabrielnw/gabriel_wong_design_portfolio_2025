HapticSense

Overview

A speculative wristband that stimulates a nerve in your wrist so you feel sensations in your fingers when you touch virtual objects in VR and AR, without gloves. I designed the device and its calibration interface, and built a clickable prototype of the setup flow.

Question

VR looks convincing, but touching things in it feels like nothing. Haptic gloves are bulky and still miss what an object feels like. Could the sense of touch come from the wrist instead of the fingertips?

The project started in The Interface, an art history course, where I read David Parisi on how touch is culturally learned and J.C.R. Licklider on human–computer symbiosis. If people learned to swipe and pinch, they can learn to read a new kind of touch. The question became what a device that teaches it would look like.

Research

In the xTouch study (Shell et al., 2022), surface electrodes at the wrist stimulated the median nerve, and participants felt the result in their hand. Seven people used it to sort virtual objects by size and softness: 64% correct against 17% chance, with size far easier to judge than softness. The study didn't measure what the sensation feels like, and it notes the nerve can shift as the wrist moves.

That set the constraints: electrodes over the median nerve, with the ulnar nerve as a speculative extension to reach the other fingers; intensity kept below the level that twitches muscles; and a band that stays in place as the wrist moves.

Interface

Nerve stimulation feels different on every body and shifts as the band moves, so the hardest design problem is not the band but the first five minutes. I designed the setup as a calibration conversation: the band asks you to flex each finger, sends a test pulse and asks if you felt it, and recalibrates if you didn't. Then you set intensity with a physical dial and immersion with a slider.

Device

Touch moves to the wrist. A row of stimulating electrodes on the underside of the band sits over the nerves at the wrist, so the hand stays free while the sensation is felt in the fingers.

A physical sense gets physical controls. Intensity sits on a dial and immersion on a slider, so you can turn the feeling down mid-session without looking away from VR.
